# Reducing unconscious bias in interviews

## Biases that distort interviews

- **Affinity bias** — warming to candidates who share your background,
  school, hobbies, or accent. Small talk about shared ground feels good and
  quietly inflates scores.
- **Halo / horns effect** — one strong (or weak) answer colors every later
  rating; a prestigious employer on the resume colors the whole interview.
- **Confirmation bias** — forming a verdict in the first minutes, then asking
  questions that confirm it (softballs for the liked candidate, gotchas for
  the doubted one).
- **Recency and contrast effects** — rating against the previous candidate
  rather than the rubric; the decent candidate after a weak one looks like a
  star.
- **Attribution bias** — reading the same behavior differently by group:
  "assertive" vs "abrasive", "confident" vs "arrogant".

## Countermeasures that work

- **Structure beats willpower.** Same core questions, same order, scored per
  competency on an anchored rubric, immediately after the interview. Most
  bias enters through improvisation.
- **Evidence-first notes.** Write down what was said and done; score from the
  notes. If a score has no supporting quote or observation, treat it as bias
  until proven otherwise.
- **Separate signal collection from decision.** Don't discuss the candidate
  with co-interviewers before each has submitted independent scores;
  the first strong opinion anchors the rest.
- **Watch your follow-up budget.** Track whether you give every candidate the
  same number of hints and probes in coding exercises — uneven scaffolding is
  a common hidden thumb on the scale.
- **Audit language in feedback.** Flag ungrounded adjectives ("culture fit",
  "not a rockstar", "great energy") and replace them with the observed
  behavior that produced the impression.
